HUMAN

The Things We Are Taught to Become

How much of the person we become was actually chosen by us, and how much was decided for us before we were old enough to question it?

We are taught things long before we understand that we are being taught.

There are so many smaller moments that have disappeared from memory. Still, something remains. A reaction, a fear, a hesitation, a strange sense of guilt over something I cannot immediately explain.

We remember so much, yet we cannot remember everything. Sometimes I wonder whether the mind keeps a different kind of record, one that does not preserve every event but carries forward the feeling those events left behind. The instructions we heard, the behaviour expected from us, the things we were praised for and the things we were made ashamed of can remain long after we have forgotten when they first entered our lives.

That is where I want to begin, because some of the things that trouble us as adults did not necessarily begin with adulthood. Their roots are often much older.

They begin when a child is first told that being a girl means something different from being a boy.

The first instructions

A girl is told to wake up early, not simply to develop discipline or learn the value of a routine, but because one day she will have a husband and in-laws to take care of. She is taught to cook, clean, wash clothes and serve food, not simply because these are useful things for every human being to know, but because she will supposedly need them when she enters another household.

She is taught how to make a round roti and told that she needs to learn properly because otherwise who will marry her. She is corrected for sleeping too long because she will not be able to wake up early for breakfast once she is married. She is told to be gentle, polite, tolerant and careful with her words because these qualities will help her adjust with her future in-laws.

The child does not yet know what marriage means. She does not even know what kind of life she wants. But she is already being prepared for it.

The lesson is not simply, “Learn how to cook because everyone needs to know how to feed themselves.” The lesson is, “Learn because one day this will be expected from you as a woman.” The distinction seems small when we are talking about a single meal or a single household task, but repeated over years, it becomes something much larger.

The child is being taught where her labour is expected to go. Her time is being organised around a future that has already been imagined for her.

And that is where the problem begins.

Cooking is not the problem. Cleaning is not the problem. Caring for a family is not the problem. There is nothing humiliating about knowing how to run a home, and there is nothing wrong with a woman choosing to devote herself to domestic life if that is genuinely what she wants.

The problem comes when a choice is removed before the person even knows there was a choice.

A skill becomes a duty. A duty becomes an expectation. An expectation becomes a measure of character. Eventually, a woman who does not perform the duty is made to feel that something is wrong with her.

When did a survival skill become proof of whether a woman was good enough?

What does “adjust” really mean?

There is another word that appears again and again in the lives of girls and women: adjust.

It is presented as a virtue. We are told that a good woman knows how to adjust, tolerate, compromise and maintain peace. Some degree of compromise is, of course, part of every human relationship. None of us can live entirely according to our own wishes, and learning to live with difference is part of becoming an adult.

But what are girls actually being taught to adjust to?

When the word is used to prepare a girl for marriage, it becomes an instruction to accept discomfort, remain silent, avoid confrontation, put other people’s needs first and endure behaviour that would not necessarily be demanded from everyone else.

Adjustment can mean maturity. It is also used to teach endurance.

That distinction matters.

A child should learn patience because patience is a human virtue. She should not be taught patience because she will one day be expected to tolerate someone else’s cruelty. She should learn kindness because kindness matters, not because she must become agreeable enough to survive in a household that does not treat her equally.

When did obedience become a virtue simply because it was easier for everyone else?

The kitchen is not the problem

I remember a discussion among friends in which one of our male friends asked what was so wrong with telling a woman to go to the kitchen and cook. He felt that women were becoming too sensitive about such statements and pointed out that cooking and serving food can be beautiful things to do for another human being.

I understood what he meant about cooking.

Cooking is beautiful. Feeding someone is an act of care. Preparing food for people we love is deeply meaningful. There is nothing inferior about domestic work, and there is certainly no reason it should be treated as beneath anyone.

But cooking itself was never the issue.

The problem is what the kitchen has come to represent.

When a girl is raised hearing that cooking is her duty, serving food is her duty, keeping the house clean is her duty, being agreeable is her duty and being meek is part of being a good woman, the kitchen stops being simply a place where food is prepared. It becomes part of the definition of womanhood that has been given to her.

That is why “go to the kitchen” is used as an insult.

The person saying it is not really discussing cooking. They are trying to put a woman back into a role they believe she should occupy. The statement carries the assumption that she has become too loud, too opinionated, too independent or too unwilling to behave according to the rules assigned to her.

The contradiction becomes even clearer when we look at how women themselves are judged through these expectations. A woman who does not know how to cook is not simply described as someone who lacks a practical skill. The judgement quickly becomes personal. “She doesn't know how to cook” turns into “What kind of woman is she?” The question is no longer about whether she knows how to prepare food. It is about whether she is considered to possess the qualities that society has decided belong to a proper woman.

The same thing happens when a woman does not instinctively serve everyone, does not rush into the kitchen, refuses to spend her life cleaning or simply chooses to sit while other people are working. Her behaviour is not always judged according to whether anything actually needs to be done. Sometimes the fact that she is sitting at all becomes the problem.

A woman visits someone's home and is expected to find something to do in the kitchen, carry plates, serve food, wash dishes, clean something or offer help even when nobody actually needs her help. During family gatherings, she is expected to remain visibly useful, while men can sit, talk, watch television or simply remain unoccupied without that same behaviour being treated as a failure of their identity.

The expectation becomes so deeply attached to womanhood that being idle itself can appear improper.

The contradiction is obvious once we look at it closely. If cooking is such a beautiful human activity, why should it belong primarily to women? If caring for others is something worth celebrating, why are boys not taught it with the same seriousness? If cleaning and cooking are valuable skills, why are girls prepared for them as obligations while boys are sometimes allowed to grow up assuming that their future wives will take care of these things?

The skill is human. The duty has been gendered. That is the difference.

Boys are given a script too

The other side of this is just as real. Boys are also raised with instructions about the kind of person they are expected to become. They are told from childhood that men must be strong, that crying is a sign of weakness, that they should not depend too much on others and that they must eventually become financially capable enough to support a family.

A boy is discouraged from entering the kitchen because cooking is considered something his future wife will handle. He is allowed to avoid household responsibilities while being simultaneously told that he must earn enough to provide for everyone else.

He is given a different burden. His worth becomes attached to his income, his strength, his ability to solve problems and his capacity to carry responsibility without appearing vulnerable.

The restrictions placed on men and women are not identical, and I do not think pretending they are would help us understand either one. The histories and consequences are different, and women have often carried particular burdens around domestic labour, marriage, obedience and dependence.

But both are being taught the same underlying lesson: your gender comes with instructions.

A boy who cries is told to “man up.” A man who struggles financially is treated as though he has failed at something deeper than employment. A man who admits that he is frightened, lonely or exhausted is made to feel that he has revealed something he should have hidden.

The contradiction becomes visible here too. The same qualities that are used to define a “good woman” are not necessarily praised when they appear in a man. A woman who sacrifices, serves, tolerates and spends her life caring for everyone else is praised for having a good heart, patience and the qualities of a good woman. A man who is excessively submissive, dependent or unable to provide is more likely to be described as weak, incapable or less manly.

The value was never only in the quality itself. The value was attached to who was performing it.

Nobody has to say, “This is the official rule.” The rule is repeated until it becomes familiar.

What happens when the rule moves inside us?

This is the part that frightens me most.

The strongest form of conditioning is not necessarily the rule someone else is still enforcing on you. It is the rule that eventually becomes an instruction inside your own mind.

I have experienced this myself. I can know that a woman has every right to speak openly, challenge expectations and refuse to make herself smaller for other people’s comfort, and still feel an uncomfortable reaction when I see someone doing exactly that. I can understand intellectually that there is nothing wrong with her behaviour and still feel a strange instinct telling me that she should be softer, quieter or more apologetic.

Where does that reaction come from?

Nobody is standing there telling me to behave differently. The instruction has already been absorbed.

This is how a social expectation becomes internalised. A woman can feel guilty for sitting down while everyone else is working. She can feel uncomfortable when a man takes over a domestic task. She can instinctively get up to collect the plates because everyone has been taught that she is the one who should do it.

A man can know that crying is not weakness and still feel ashamed when tears come.

The mind can change before the body does.

We can tell ourselves that something is wrong, irrational or outdated, and yet the body can respond as though we have violated some deeply important rule. A change that should feel ordinary can arrive with hesitation, anxiety, shame or a racing heartbeat, as though we are doing something forbidden simply because it is unfamiliar.

That experience is difficult to explain because the rule no longer exists in the room. Nobody is threatening us. Nobody is stopping us. Yet our own nervous system can seem to resist the change.

I do not know whether every such reaction can simply be described or resolved as “unlearning.” Some beliefs require years of conscious work to examine, and some patterns are so deeply embedded in our emotional lives that changing them takes more than intellectual understanding. We have to learn new ways of responding to ourselves before those old reactions begin to loosen their grip.

That is why declaring ourselves free from inherited expectations is rarely enough. We can know, with complete conviction, that we owe no one our silence and still feel our pulse race the moment we choose to speak.

The rules do not have to be written

We often talk about society as though it were a separate force standing outside us, imposing rules on everyone else.

But who exactly is society?

Society is the parents and relatives who tell a girl that she must learn to cook before marriage. It is the father telling his son not to cry. It is the relative telling a girl to lower her voice. It is the person laughing when a man washes the dishes and asking why the women in the house are not doing it.

Society is the person who says a woman should have a big heart, should adjust, should obey and should remain pleasant no matter what is happening around her. Society is also the woman who repeats those same expectations because they were once taught to her as the rules of survival.

This is one of the most uncomfortable parts of the cycle. Women themselves often become the people who enforce these expectations on other women. A mother teaches her daughter to cook because she was taught that a woman must know how to run a home. A grandmother criticises a granddaughter for not knowing household work because she has learned to see those skills as part of proper womanhood. A female relative sees another woman sitting while the household work is being done and asks why she is not helping.

The same expectation is passed from woman to woman, sometimes with no man in the room at all.

There is something unsettling about that continuity.

The women enforcing these expectations were not born believing them. They were taught them too. They grew up hearing what a good woman should be, watching women work while men rested, seeing obedience praised and ambition questioned, and learning that domestic usefulness was connected to dignity. By the time they reach adulthood, the rule can feel so natural that enforcing it seems like teaching rather than control.

The people who pass these expectations down are not always trying to harm the children they are raising. Sometimes they genuinely believe that they are protecting them. A parent who tells a daughter to learn how to cook believes they are preparing her for the world. A relative who tells a son to become strong believes they are helping him survive it.

But preparation for life is not the same thing as preparation for a predetermined life.

At some point, we have to ask whether we are giving a child tools or giving them instructions about who they must become.

The cost is more than emotional

These expectations also have consequences that go beyond hurt feelings or arguments about whether a statement is offensive.

Domestic work takes time. Cooking, cleaning, planning meals, shopping for food, caring for children, looking after elderly family members and maintaining a household all require labour. When those responsibilities are automatically assigned to women, the cost is not simply that they become tired.

Time that goes into unpaid domestic work is time that cannot simultaneously be spent on education, rest, career development, creative work, friendships, travel or simply doing nothing.

This is one reason the idea of a “second shift” matters. A woman spends the day working for a salary and then returns home to another set of responsibilities that are treated as so normal that nobody even describes them as work.

The fact that no money changes hands does not make the labour disappear.

And once that labour is treated as a woman's natural responsibility, asking for it to be shared is itself treated as a complaint rather than a question of fairness.

The issue is no longer merely who cooked dinner. It becomes a question of whose time is considered available to everyone else.

Why does it survive?

We look around and see that the world has changed enormously in many ways. Women study, work, travel, earn money and live independently. Men are more openly involved in childcare and domestic life in many families. Conversations about equality are far more visible than they once were.

Yet many of the old expectations about what women should do and what men should be have survived the changes around us.

We still see those expectations in households, films, television serials, advertisements and social media. Women are repeatedly shown standing in kitchens, preparing meals, setting tables, washing clothes, cleaning homes and caring for everyone around them, while men are shown sitting in front of televisions, returning from work or occupying the position of the person being served. Food advertisements often place women at the centre of cooking and serving. Dishwashing and detergent advertisements repeatedly place women in front of sinks and piles of clothes, demonstrating how effectively a product can perform the household work that has already been assigned to them.

Television serials repeat the same expectations through stories about what makes a woman good. The woman who takes care of her in-laws, cooks for the entire family, keeps the house running, sacrifices her own desires and remains quiet and accommodating is often presented as virtuous. The woman who wants a career, wants to travel, wants independence or refuses to make her entire identity revolve around domestic sacrifice is often presented as selfish, difficult or misguided, and the story eventually teaches her that choosing ambition over the role assigned to her was a mistake.

These messages do not remain on a screen. They return to the household, where children are watching them. A girl sees women being praised for the same duties she is already being taught to perform. A boy sees the same division of labour repeated around him. What was first taught at home is reinforced by the culture outside it.

And the people creating that culture did not grow up outside society. The writer creating the character, the director deciding what a “good woman” should look like, the advertiser deciding who belongs in the kitchen and the person creating an image of a modern family were all children once. They grew up around the same expectations. They were told what women should do, what men should be, where each belonged and which behaviours deserved approval.

They grow up, enter adulthood and carry those ideas with them. Some question them. Others reproduce them without even recognising how much of what they create has been shaped by what they were taught to believe was normal.

Even AI-generated videos that depict supposedly modern or futuristic lives can reproduce the same familiar roles. The technology changes, the setting changes, the clothes change, but the woman is still placed in the kitchen while the man sits elsewhere, works or occupies the position of provider. We can imagine flying cars, intelligent machines and cities that do not yet exist while still imagining women and men through roles that belong to generations before us.

That is what makes the problem so deeply rooted. The message does not travel in only one direction. Childhood shapes the adults who later shape culture, and that culture returns to shape another generation of children.

Why does that happen?

Because social rules do not survive only through deliberate agreement. They survive through repetition.

A parent repeats what they were taught because they believe it will prepare a daughter. A father teaches his son to suppress emotion because that was what strength looked like when he was growing up. A relative makes a joke about a man washing dishes, everyone laughs, and the message survives another generation.

No one has to sit down and decide to preserve the system. It simply keeps moving from one ordinary moment into another.

That is why familiarity can be dangerous. Something can be repeated for generations and still be unjust. Something can be old and still deserve to be questioned. Something can feel natural simply because we have never experienced a world without it.

When does choice become real?

There is another question I think is important because it prevents this discussion from becoming an argument against domestic life itself.

What if a woman genuinely wants to stay at home? What if she loves cooking, raising children, managing a household or caring for her family? What if a man genuinely enjoys being the primary provider?

There is nothing wrong with any of those choices.

The problem is not the role itself. The problem is whether the person could have chosen differently without being made to feel that they had failed their gender.

A woman who chooses domestic life is making a meaningful choice only when another life was also available to her. A man who chooses to stay home with his children is making a meaningful choice only when doing so does not automatically make him less of a man in everyone else’s eyes.

Real freedom must include the freedom to choose the traditional path as well as the freedom to leave it.

Otherwise, we have simply replaced one compulsory role with another.

What are we teaching children to become?

I think about the small girls I still see being prepared for marriage long before they are prepared for the world.

They are told to wake up early because otherwise they will not adjust with their future in-laws. They are taught to prepare proper meals because they will one day need to cook for their family. They are told to keep the house clean because that is what a good woman does. They are encouraged to be gentle and have fewer opinions because being too outspoken makes it difficult for them to adjust.

And I keep wondering why we are preparing them to fit into someone else’s household before teaching them how to build a life of their own.

Teach her to cook, but teach her because she deserves to know how to feed herself.

Teach her to clean because she deserves to know how to take care of the place where she lives.

Teach her to be polite because kindness is a virtue, not because she will one day need to tolerate someone's taunts.

Teach her to think, speak, question and disagree. Teach her how large the world is. Show her what she can become before telling her what she should be.

Teach boys the same things. Let them learn how to cook and clean without treating those abilities as feminine. Let them understand that their identity does not disappear when they are unemployed. Let them know that earning is one part of a life, not proof of their humanity. Let them cry without turning it into a lesson about weakness.

Children need preparation for life. They do not need a predetermined script for it.

What we choose to pass on

I do not think every tradition needs to be destroyed. Our inheritance is not made only of harmful ideas. It contains care, language, memory, responsibility, culture and ways of belonging to one another.

The responsibility is to examine what we inherited rather than passing all of it forward automatically.

We can keep the value of caring for family without making women responsible for it by default. We can keep the importance of financial responsibility without making men believe that their worth is measured by their income. We can teach patience without teaching women to tolerate mistreatment. We can teach strength without teaching men to hide their pain. We can teach cooking and cleaning without giving those skills a gender.

The question is not whether children should learn how to function inside a household. Of course they should. The question is why, so often, we begin assigning those household responsibilities according to gender before the child has even begun imagining the person they might become.

And perhaps that is the part of this that stays with me most.

A child is born with a world of possibilities ahead of them, and before they are old enough to understand what those possibilities are, we begin narrowing them.

A girl is prepared to become a wife before she has been encouraged to imagine a career. A boy is prepared to become a provider before he has been given permission to understand his own vulnerability. A girl is taught that her usefulness lies in how well she serves others. A boy is taught that his worth lies in how much he can provide.

Both can grow into adults who spend years trying to separate their own voices from the voices they inherited.

That is why I do not think this is a small social issue.

It is about what we do to a human being before they are old enough to defend themselves from the ideas we place inside them. It is about time, labour, identity, freedom, shame, expectation and the right to become something that was not already decided for us.

We cannot change the world simply by telling children that men and women are equal. We have to look at what we are actually teaching them through the responsibilities we assign, the behaviour we reward, the emotions we permit and the futures we prepare them for.

Because society is not somewhere else. It is in our kitchens, our conversations, our jokes, our marriages, our films, our families and our expectations. It is in the things we tell children when they are too young to know that they have the right to question us.

And if we are the ones teaching the script, then we are also the ones who can decide whether it needs to be passed on.

The real question is not what kind of woman we want a girl to become, or what kind of man we want a boy to become. It is whether we can give them enough freedom to become human beings before we start asking them to perform the roles attached to their gender.

Because a child should not have to spend adulthood unlearning the life they were trained to live.

They should have been given the chance to discover it for themselves.
