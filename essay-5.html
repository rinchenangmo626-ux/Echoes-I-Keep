MEMORY

The Architecture of a World We Carry

Sometimes the mind builds a world that reality could not give us.

Why do we sometimes wish our lives were like a film?

Not the usual kind, with a hero and a heroine, dramatic entrances and perfectly timed endings. I mean something quieter. A life with a soft song playing somewhere in the background, changing with every emotion. Moonlight falling gently across a room. A soft breeze moving through the hair. Rain arriving slowly, making everything around us feel almost unreal.

There are moments when that alternative, almost cinematic life seems extraordinarily beautiful.

I have always loved moonlight and a soft breeze, perhaps because they carry something with them that I cannot completely explain. They remind me of certain days from my childhood, particularly the part of it I spent at my maternal grandmother's house.

Sometimes I wonder whether the worlds I imagine now are actually fragments of those days trying to return.

Perhaps somewhere inside the mind there are memories we can no longer fully retrieve. We may not remember what happened, who said what, or exactly how a particular afternoon looked. Yet somehow, the feeling remains.

Or perhaps it is not memory alone.

Perhaps the mind takes fragments of what we have lived, mixes them with what we long for, and creates somewhere new.

Whatever it is, I seem to carry another world with me.

It is not always perfectly happy. That may be what makes it feel strangely real. There are emotions there too, including sadness, longing, wonder, fear, affection. The difference is that I seem to have some control over how those emotions move. I can create something, remove something, change something, return somewhere, leave somewhere.

It is almost as though I am holding a remote control over a world that exists only inside me.

And sometimes I wonder whether this is something all human beings do in their own way.

Perhaps everyone carries a private world that nobody else can completely enter, a world assembled from memories, wishes, imagination, fears, possibilities and fragments of things we have seen.

Maybe we create places that do not exist anywhere outside our minds.

Maybe we become versions of ourselves that reality never allowed us to become.

And perhaps there is something revealing about the worlds we construct.

Maybe what we repeatedly create is connected to what we deeply yearn for.

The world inside the world

There are times when we don't want conversation.

We don't want to explain ourselves.

We don't even necessarily want anyone around us.

Not because we have stopped caring about human beings, but because sometimes the outside world becomes exhausting.

There are too many voices.

Too many expectations.

Too many opinions about how we should behave, what we should want, what we should become.

At such times, turning inward can feel less like escaping life and more like returning to ourselves.

Perhaps when the outside world becomes too loud, the mind does not always need another person.

Sometimes it needs a room of its own.

A place where nobody is telling it what to feel.

A place where it can simply exist.

Maybe that is why I have started understanding certain poets differently.

When I was younger, I would read poems about rain, leaves, moonlight, birds, rivers and shadows and wonder why someone would write so much about something so small.

Now I think I understand the impulse differently.

Perhaps they were not merely describing the world.

Perhaps they were recreating it.

Taking something ordinary and giving it another life inside language.

Turning a leaf into a thought.

Turning rain into an emotion.

Turning a landscape into a place where the mind could breathe.

I cannot know what every poet felt, of course. But I understand the impulse differently now.

Sometimes the world outside is too loud, and the mind begins making its own music.

We write.

We paint.

We imagine.

We compose.

We turn ordinary things into symbols.

Maybe we are not always escaping reality.

Maybe sometimes we are trying to create a version of reality in which something inside us can finally breathe.

The places memory leaves behind

For me, birdsong and rain have always carried something with them.

So does a mild breeze.

So does the sound of clean water moving through a stream.

Sometimes a particular atmosphere arrives and suddenly I am somewhere else.

I am back at my grandmother's house.

I remember sleeping beneath the open sky, the tall poplars and willows standing around us, the stars appearing through their branches as though they were playing hide-and-seek.

I remember the rain falling slowly, making its own quiet music.

I remember birds calling in the distance and the sound of running water.

There was something about those nights that felt larger than ordinary life.

I was too young to understand any of it in the way I understand things now.

Perhaps I did not even think of those moments as beautiful.

I simply lived them.

That may be why they have remained so powerful.

Today, when it rains, something in me responds before I have consciously remembered anything.

A sound of running water can make me feel as though I have been transported somewhere.

A breeze can suddenly carry the atmosphere of a place I haven't seen in years.

The place becomes an emotion.

The weather becomes a doorway.

The sound of water becomes a passage between two versions of myself.

Perhaps that is why some memories don't behave like photographs.

They behave more like emotions waiting for the right moment to return.

Psychology gives us part of an explanation. Autobiographical memory is not a perfect recording of the past; remembering involves reconstructing experiences through the perspective, emotions and knowledge we have in the present. Sensory and emotional cues can also bring personal memories to mind without deliberate effort.

Perhaps that is why we can remember the feeling of a place even when many of its details have disappeared.

The facts may become less accessible.

The feeling can remain.

Would I recognise it if I went back?

There is a question I return to sometimes.

Suppose I could go back.

Not in imagination.

Actually return.

The same house.

The same trees.

The same sky.

The same rain.

But I would take the mind I have today with me.

Would I experience it in the same way?

Would the stars still surprise me?

Would the moon still feel like a companion?

Would the poplar trees still seem enormous?

Would the sound of the stream still feel like another world?

Or would I notice things the child I was could not?

The worries of adults.

The imperfections of the place.

The ordinary difficulties that children don't always see.

Perhaps I would notice how much work went into the life I experienced as magical.

Perhaps I would understand sadness that I was too young to recognise.

Perhaps I would discover that the world I remember as peaceful contained its own complications.

And perhaps the memory would change.

That possibility almost frightens me.

Because there is something precious about not knowing everything.

Maybe childhood has its own way of looking at the world.

A child does not necessarily need to escape into an imagined reality because reality itself can still feel like one.

When we are young, we may not need to invent the magic.

We are already inside it.

As we grow older, we begin noticing things we could not see before.

We understand motives.

We recognise tension in a room.

We become conscious of time, responsibility, expectations, loss and all the invisible structures surrounding human life.

Perhaps growing up gives us greater understanding.

But it also changes what we can see.

Perhaps the world has not become less beautiful.

Perhaps we have simply become less available to its beauty.

Perhaps we build what we miss

Maybe our imagined worlds reveal something about what we yearn for.

If someone repeatedly imagines travelling somewhere far away, perhaps freedom matters deeply to them.

If someone imagines a quiet house surrounded by trees, perhaps peace matters.

If someone repeatedly imagines being understood by another person, perhaps recognition matters.

And perhaps when I create my own strange, beautiful worlds, they are telling me something too.

Maybe I am looking for peace.

Maybe I am looking for freedom from noise.

Maybe I am looking for a version of myself that existed before I learned all the rules about how a person is supposed to behave.

Because while growing up, we are constantly given instructions.

How to speak.

How to sit.

How to behave.

What is respectable.

What is embarrassing.

What is acceptable.

What is successful.

What we should want.

What we should become.

Some of these lessons are necessary. Growing up means learning how to live with other people and with the world.

But somewhere along the way, perhaps some of us lose small pieces of ourselves while learning how to become acceptable to everyone else.

A certain spontaneity.

A certain curiosity.

A certain freedom.

A certain way of seeing beauty.

A certain version of ourselves that existed before we became so aware of how we were being observed.

Perhaps the alternate self we imagine is not an entirely different person.

Perhaps it is a version of ourselves reminding us of something we once had.

Or perhaps it is a version of ourselves we have not yet become.

Maybe the two are not always separate.

The strange contradiction of wanting to disappear

There are moments when I want to close my eyes and simply go somewhere else.

Not disappear.

That distinction matters.

Part of me wants to withdraw completely from the noise, while another part of me wants desperately to remain here and experience life differently.

I want solitude, but not loneliness.

I want silence, but not emptiness.

I want freedom, but not meaninglessness.

I want to be unseen for a while, but I also want to be understood.

Perhaps many of us carry these contradictions.

We want the world to know our story, and yet there are days when we want nobody to know anything about us.

We want connection, and then we become exhausted by people.

We want to express ourselves completely, and then we want to close the door and retreat into a world where nobody can reach us.

Maybe being human means carrying these opposing desires without always resolving them.

Perhaps the private world is where some of those contradictions are temporarily allowed to coexist.

There, we don't have to choose.

We can be alone without feeling abandoned.

We can feel deeply without explaining ourselves.

We can create without being judged.

We can exist without performing.

Perhaps that is why the inward world can feel so intimate.

Nobody else has complete authority over it.

The life we create behind our eyes

The strange thing about these alternate realities is that they do not always arrive deliberately.

Sometimes I try to imagine something beautiful and nothing comes.

The darkness of reality can be so overwhelming that even imagination seems to lose its freedom.

And yet, at other times, it arrives effortlessly.

Rain.

Wind.

The sound of water.

Moonlight.

And suddenly the door opens.

I am somewhere else.

The world is constructed again.

Not perfectly.

Not necessarily happily.

But according to some strange internal logic that I don't fully understand.

Perhaps that is one of the most mysterious things about the human mind.

It can take fragments of the past, fragments of desire, fragments of imagination and emotion, and create something that feels almost like a place.

A place that has never existed.

And yet, while we are inside it, it can feel more intimate than many places that actually do.

Maybe that is why dreams, stories, poems, paintings and music have followed human beings for so long.

We are creatures who don't simply live in the physical world.

We interpret it.

Reconstruct it.

Reimagine it.

Sometimes we even build another one.

Perhaps the child is still there

I sometimes wonder whether the child who loved the rain is still somewhere inside me.

Not literally waiting to come back.

But present in the strange happiness that appears when rain touches the earth.

Present in the way a breeze can suddenly change my mood.

Present in the way running water can make an ordinary moment feel unfamiliar and beautiful.

Perhaps those sensations are not merely reminders.

Perhaps they are small continuities between the person I was and the person I became.

We lose so many versions of ourselves while growing up.

Some disappear because life changes.

Some because circumstances require us to become harder than we once were.

Some simply become quiet.

Maybe imagination gives those quieter versions somewhere to exist.

Maybe the person we become in our private worlds is not entirely imaginary.

Maybe somewhere beneath everything we were taught to become, there remains something that was already ours.

Something natural.

Something that existed before the world began telling us what needed to be changed.

I don't know if that is true.

But I like the possibility.

The memory I choose not to disturb

And perhaps this is why I don't actually want to return to my childhood with the mind I have today.

I would rather keep it the way it lives inside me.

Not because it was perfect.

I know it probably wasn't.

But because memory has given me something reality cannot give me anymore: a place I can return to without changing what happened.

If I went back with everything I know now, perhaps I would see too much.

Perhaps the extraordinary would become ordinary.

The poplar trees would simply be trees.

The stars would simply be stars.

The rain would simply be rain.

The stream would simply be water moving downhill.

And I am not sure I want to risk that.

I would rather keep the child beneath those stars.

Let the trees remain enormous.

Let the moon remain a quiet companion.

Let the rain remain a lullaby.

Let that little world remain untouched by the consciousness I carry now.

Because perhaps one of the strange privileges of growing older is that we cannot return to every beautiful moment, but we can carry the way it made us feel.

And perhaps the present deserves more attention than we usually give it.

One day, these very days may become the memories we long to return to.

The conversations we are having.

The rooms we are sitting in.

The walks we are taking.

The ordinary evenings we barely notice.

Some future version of ourselves may remember them through a particular sound, a smell, a quality of light, and wonder why we did not understand their beauty while we were living them.

Perhaps the present is already becoming a memory.

So maybe the answer is not to become children again.

We cannot return to the exact childhood we remember.

We cannot become the exact people we were then.

But perhaps we can recover something more valuable than the past itself: the ability to be present inside our own lives.

To watch the moon without demanding an explanation.

To listen to the wind without turning it into a metaphor.

To notice birdsong without deciding whether it is beautiful enough.

To sit somewhere quietly and let a moment exist without immediately asking what it means.

We cannot go backward.

But perhaps we can learn to look again.

The same wind is still flowing.

The water is still moving.

The moon is still there.

Maybe the world did not become entirely different.

Maybe we simply became different people looking at it.

And perhaps, every once in a while, something ordinary reminds us that the old world was never completely lost.

It was carried forward.

Inside us.

Not as a place we can return to, but as a way of seeing that we can choose, sometimes, to recover.

Perhaps that is the real architecture of the world we carry.
