The Spark of Curiosity: Dr. Andrew Brown

Sometimes a person we have never met can leave an impression that feels strangely familiar.

There was nothing unusual about the day I found Everwood.

I was doing what I had done on many other days: reading, studying languages, watching movies, and moving from one thing to another without much of a plan. I had watched more films and series than I could count, often skipping from one to another until something made me want to stay.

Then I came across Everwood.

There was a stillness to it. It did not feel like the kind of drama that needed to constantly pull the viewer from one dramatic event to another. It had room for silence, grief, and the ordinary difficulties of being human. So I stayed.

And then there was Dr. Andrew Brown.

I noticed his voice first. There was a calmness in it that I found difficult to explain. His stillness, his vulnerability, and the quiet way he carried himself created a strange sense of familiarity, almost as if some part of me already knew him.

In the series, Dr. Brown is a widowed doctor who moves his two children to Everwood after the death of his wife. He tries to build a new life there while carrying the weight of grief and trying to remain a father to two children who are themselves struggling with the loss of their mother.

He continues to speak to his deceased wife as though she were still beside him. He tells her about his children, his difficulties, his decisions, and the ordinary events of his life. What struck me was not simply that he imagined her presence, but how naturally that presence remained woven into his life. Death had ended her physical presence, but it had not ended the relationship he carried with her.

His daughter initially did not understand. To her, it was strange, perhaps even worrying. But eventually, she began to understand what she was seeing. It was grief.

There is one moment I keep returning to.

His daughter finds him once again speaking to his deceased wife as though she were still there. When he realizes that she has been watching him, he becomes self conscious, almost caught off guard, and tells her that they need to talk about it.

She tells him that she knows what is wrong with him.

In a quiet voice, he asks, “You do?”

She answers, “You have a distraught heart.”

His eyes fill with tears, perhaps because his daughter has finally understood the grief he has been carrying. For a moment, he becomes completely vulnerable in front of her, and they embrace. Something about that moment stayed with me.

I remember feeling several things at once: sadness, relief, recognition, tenderness, loneliness, and something else I still cannot quite name.

Perhaps what moved me was not grief itself, but the moment in which grief was finally recognized by another person.

A person can carry something inside for a very long time without knowing how to explain it. Then someone sees it. They understand something that has been difficult to say aloud. For a brief moment, the burden becomes shared.

That scene made me think about how much of human life exists beneath what we can see.

We meet people every day, but we rarely see the whole of them. We see their expressions, their habits, their confidence or hesitation, their kindness or anger. What we cannot see immediately are the years that came before them, the experiences, losses, relationships, memories, fears, and small moments that gradually shaped the person standing in front of us.

Perhaps this is one of the reasons certain people feel strangely familiar.

I have watched countless characters before Dr. Brown. I have been emotionally attached to fictional characters and built imaginary worlds around them. That, in itself, is not unusual. Stories give us places to retreat to, especially when ordinary life becomes tiring.

But Everwood was different.

Even before I fully understood Dr. Andy Brown, I had already felt something through his voice. The calmness of it, the stillness, the way he spoke, it created a feeling I could not explain. As I continued watching, I found more of the qualities that moved me: vulnerability, kindness, wisdom, fatherhood, grief, and the quiet effort to keep going.

I had experienced emotional connections with characters before. But I had never experienced this particular kind of connection.

And then I looked up Treat Williams, the actor who brought him to life.

That was when I discovered that he had died in 2023, following a motorcycle accident.

I had found a person through his work at the same time I discovered that the person himself was already gone.

I found that unexpectedly painful.

I had never met him. I knew almost nothing about his life before discovering his work. Yet the news of his death felt strangely personal. I knew that a part of what I was feeling came from the character he had played, and perhaps from the story itself. I also knew that there was something about his presence that had reached me before I had any explanation for it.

Psychology has language for some forms of this experience. Research on narrative transportation describes how deeply people can become absorbed in fictional worlds, while research on parasocial relationships examines the one sided sense of closeness people can develop with figures they encounter through media. Such connections can also help explain why the death of someone we never personally knew can still produce genuine grief.

But those explanations only take me so far.

They can describe the phenomenon. They cannot tell me why one particular voice reaches one particular person at one particular moment in life.

I do not know whether it was the character, the performance, the voice, something familiar in his mannerisms, or something within myself that I recognized through him.

Perhaps I do not need to know completely.

Some human experiences become less meaningful when we force them into a complete explanation.

What I do know is that the experience changed something in me.

It made me think about the strange ways human beings remain connected to one another without ever meeting. A person can live in one place, at one point in history, and create something that reaches someone they could never have imagined.

That thought brought me to another question: what remains of a person after they are gone?

A body does not remain forever. A voice eventually becomes a recording. A photograph becomes an image of someone who is no longer there.

And yet, something can remain.

A book can reach someone who was not born when its author died. A painting can speak to someone the painter never met. A performance can move someone decades after the person who gave it has left the world.

Perhaps this is one of the quieter ways a human life continues.

We often think of leaving something behind in terms of achievement, wealth, property, or recognition. But there is another kind of trace: the effect one human being leaves on another.

Treat Williams did not know that I existed. He could never have known that, years after his death, a stranger would watch his work and feel something strong enough to make her question human connection, grief, memory, mortality, and her own desire to create something that would remain.

That realization became important to me.

For a long time, I had wanted to write. I had questions about memory, art, death, existence, human behaviour, and the invisible experiences that shape us. But my thoughts existed everywhere at once. I had no place to gather them.

Then this happened.

I began thinking about what it means to leave evidence of having been here, not necessarily evidence that makes a person famous, but something that allows their presence to continue through what they created.

I do not want to disappear as though I had never existed.

Not because I believe everyone must remember me. I know that is impossible. Most people who have ever lived are now unknown to us, despite having had entire lives filled with love, fear, struggle, work, laughter, and ordinary days that mattered deeply to them.

What matters to me is the act of leaving something behind.

A thought.

A piece of writing.

A work.

A question.

Something that carries a small trace of the person who created it.

Perhaps that is why I began this publication.

Echoes I Keep began, in part, with that strange encounter, with a character who felt familiar, a voice that felt strangely comforting, and the discovery that the person behind it was already gone.

I do not know exactly what that connection was.

Maybe it was simply the way a story met me at a particular point in my life. Maybe it was the performance. Maybe it was something I recognized in the character, or something within myself that I had not yet learned to name.

I am not sure.

But perhaps that uncertainty is part of the reason I want to write.

There are so many things we experience without fully understanding them. We notice them, carry them, and sometimes years later realize that they were quietly changing the way we saw the world.

Maybe human beings are full of such invisible connections.

Maybe we spend our lives collecting them without realizing it.

And perhaps, sometimes, one of them becomes an echo we keep.
